    Datasets

    CBIS-DDSM (main dataset)#

    CBIS-DDSM is a free, public set of scanned film mammograms. Each lesion has a biopsy-confirmed label (benign or malignant) and a density grade from a radiologist.

    Where to get it

    • Official release (DICOM): CBIS-DDSM on The Cancer Imaging Archive · dataset DOI 10.7937/K9/TCIA.2016.7O02S9CY
    • JPEG version used by this code (folders csv/ and jpeg/): CBIS-DDSM: Breast Cancer Image Dataset on Kaggle

    What was used

    Count
    Lesion cases 3,568
    Mammogram images 3,103
    Patients 1,566
    Malignant / benign 1,457 / 2,111
    Density A / B / C / D / missing 536 / 1,373 / 1,103 / 554 / 2

    Turning density into two groups#

    The method needs density as a yes/no value: "dense" or "not dense".

    • The split point is the middle (median) grade, B. Grades B, C and D count as dense; grade A and the two missing cases count as not dense.
    • This differs from the usual clinical split (A/B versus C/D). The two agree on 61.5% of cases and differ only for grade B, so the experiments are also repeated with the clinical split and with the full A–D scale.
    • In the original data, density hardly relates to cancer at all. The cancer rate stays between 0.38 and 0.42 for every grade, and the correlation is close to zero under every definition (|r| ≤ 0.022).

    Adding a known shortcut#

    Because the real data has almost no density–cancer link, one is added on purpose, only to the training data:

    • Training cases are sampled so that 85% of cancers are dense and 85% of benign cases are not dense. This gives a correlation of about r ≈ 0.6.
    • Validation and test data are not changed, and all splits keep each patient's images together.
    • Sampling roughly halves the training set (about 1,996 → 983 cases) and raises the share of cancers in it to 78.6%, compared with 40.8% overall. All methods get exactly the same training data.

    Why add a shortcut on purpose?

    Without a real shortcut in the data, you cannot tell a method that works from a method that had nothing to fix. Adding a known shortcut to training only, and testing on untouched data, makes the difference measurable. The shortcut-strength study also runs the experiment with weaker shortcuts and with none.

    INbreast (second dataset)#

    INbreast is a smaller set of digital mammograms from one hospital in Portugal. It shows whether the findings carry over from film to modern digital images.

    Where to get it: INbreast is shared by its authors on request. The dataset is described in Moreira et al., INbreast: toward a full-field digital mammographic database, Academic Radiology, 2012. Contact the dataset authors through that paper to request access.

    What was used

    • 192 images from 102 patients (50 malignant, 142 benign) with a complete assessment, a density value and an image file.
    • BI-RADS 3 ("probably benign") images are left out because they have no clear label.
    • INbreast has no biopsy results, so labels come from the radiologist's BI-RADS assessment: 4–6 count as malignant, 1–2 as benign.
    • Density uses the clinical split (57 dense, 135 not dense). Here too the natural correlation is close to zero (r = −0.022).
    • After the shortcut is added, only about 72 training images remain, about 8 of them malignant.
